# Live Demo Script — AI-Powered Criminal Network Analysis

**Total time: ~5-6 minutes.** Practice this a couple of times so the clicking feels natural — you want to be talking while you click, not narrating your own mouse movements.

---

## 1. Open with the problem (30 seconds)

> "Investigators today deal with data scattered across police reports, phone records, bank transactions, and social media — all in different formats, in different systems. Manually cross-referencing all of that is slow, and it's easy to miss connections that aren't obvious from any single source. We built a system that does this automatically."

---

## 2. Set up the story before revealing the result (30 seconds)

> "For this demo, we generated realistic synthetic data — fake police reports, call records, bank transactions, and social media activity for five people. We deliberately hid one person, Sanjay Gupta, from every single written police report. He only shows up in phone, financial, and social data. The question is: can our system find him anyway?"

*(This framing matters — you're telling the audience what to watch for before you show them the answer.)*

---

## 3. Open the dashboard (30 seconds)

Double-click `dashboard/index.html`.

> "This is the investigator-facing dashboard. No installation, runs in any browser. On the left, every person in the network, ranked by how structurally important they are — not just how many reports mention them. In the center, the actual network graph. On the right, details on whoever we select."

Point out the **red banner at the top**:
> "The system already found something — 16 calls between two people in a 72-hour window. That's flagged automatically, before we've clicked anything."

---

## 4. The reveal (60 seconds) — this is the core moment

Click on **Sanjay Gupta** in the sidebar.

> "Here's Sanjay Gupta. Remember — zero mentions in any police report. But look: he's ranked #2 in the entire network for 'bridge score' — that's betweenness centrality, a measure of who connects otherwise-separate groups of people."

Point to the connections list on the right:
> "Here's why: he's tied to Rahul Sharma through 16 phone calls AND a financial transaction AND a social media friendship. Three independent sources, all pointing to the same connection. No single source alone would have made him stand out this much — it's the fusion of all three that surfaced him."

Point to the flagged pattern box:
> "And this call pattern — 16 calls in 72 hours — is automatically flagged for human review. Not as an accusation, just as something worth a closer look."

---

## 5. Show the graph interactivity (30 seconds)

> "The whole network is interactive — click anyone, and their connections light up in the graph, so an investigator can visually trace how a case connects to people who were never explicitly named."

Click one or two other people briefly (Priya Nair, Rahul Sharma) to show it's not a one-off.

---

## 6. Show the technical depth, briefly (30-45 seconds)

*(Only if presenting to a technical audience/judge — skip for a general audience.)*

> "Behind this: NLP extraction pulls entities out of report text, fuzzy matching resolves name variants to one identity, and the connections form a graph analyzed with the same centrality algorithms used in network science — degree, betweenness, PageRank — plus automatic community detection to find likely groups. We also have a backend API so this could plug into other tools, not just this one dashboard."

If asked "is this real data" — be direct:
> "No — entirely synthetic, generated by us, specifically so we could control and verify the 'hidden connection' scenario. The pipeline itself works the same way on real data."

---

## 7. Close (20 seconds)

> "The core idea: no single record tells the whole story, but connecting them does. That's what this system automates — turning scattered records into one picture, and surfacing the people and patterns a manual review would take much longer to find, or might miss entirely."

---

## Anticipated Questions & Honest Answers

**"How accurate is the NER/extraction?"**
> "Good but not perfect — off-the-shelf models occasionally misclassify names, which is why we always keep a human in the loop for final decisions. We saw this firsthand during testing."

**"What stops this from just accusing random people?"**
> "Every score is a signal for review, not a conclusion. We deliberately used language like 'flagged for review' throughout, never anything accusatory. A human always makes the final call."

**"Does this work on real crime data?"**
> "The pipeline architecture is data-source agnostic — same code would work on real data, subject to proper legal authority, data governance, and privacy safeguards. We used synthetic data specifically to avoid any of those concerns for this demo."

**"How does this scale beyond 5 people?"**
> "The algorithms (NetworkX, RapidFuzz) are the same ones used in production systems at much larger scale. For real deployment, you'd swap our in-memory storage for a proper graph database like Neo4j, which we scoped in our architecture slides."
